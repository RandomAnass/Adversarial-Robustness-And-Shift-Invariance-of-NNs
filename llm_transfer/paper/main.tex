\begin{abstract}
A model that assigns the same label to shifted views of an image is often called robust, and this
shift-invariance is treated as a route to adversarial robustness. We show, on frozen vision-language
image encoders, that shift-invariance does not order adversarial robustness while a threat-matched
margin-to-Lipschitz ratio \etaL{} does. The ratio is the mean classification margin on clean images
divided by the mean dual-norm of the input gradient of that margin; it needs no attack to compute and
is unchanged by rescaling a model's logits. Our powered result is per image, within a fixed encoder,
where adversarial training cannot act as a confound: the per-image \etaL{} orders the per-image
certified adversarial radius at Spearman $+0.74$ to $+0.85$, more than twice as strongly as per-image
shift-consistency ($+0.27$ to $+0.37$), and the gap is significant in a paired test. Across a panel of
frozen encoders \etaL{} also ranks tower-level AutoAttack robustness, though this axis is limited by
how few adversarially robust encoders exist. We further show that the apparent link between
shift-invariance and robustness is a weak-attack artifact: on every non-robust encoder, single-step
FGSM reports 16 to 61\% robust accuracy that AutoAttack erases to zero, so a recent claim that
architectural invariance confers robustness without adversarial training does not survive a strong
attack. Throughout we use AutoAttack as the arbiter and check for gradient masking.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
A model that assigns the same output to a shifted view of an image is often described as robust, and
this shift-invariance is frequently offered as an explanation for, or a route to, adversarial
robustness. The two properties are not the same. Invariance is about ignoring changes that should not
matter; adversarial robustness is about not being flipped by a small worst-case change. This paper
asks whether shift-invariance predicts adversarial robustness in frozen vision-language image
encoders, and finds that it does not, while a simple margin-based quantity does.

That quantity is a threat-matched margin-to-Lipschitz ratio. For an input $x$ with correct label $y$,
let the margin be $M(x) = f_y(x) - \max_{j\neq y} f_j(x)$, the gap between the correct logit and the
strongest competitor. The per-image ratio is $M(x) / \lVert \nabla_x M(x) \rVert_q$: the margin divided
by how fast it changes when the input is perturbed, measured in the norm dual to the attack ($q=1$ for
an $\ell_\infty$ attack, $q=2$ for an $\ell_2$ attack). A first-order argument gives the certificate
that the adversarial radius of $x$ is at least this per-image ratio, because a perturbation of size
$\delta$ moves the margin by at most $\lVert \nabla_x M(x) \rVert_q \lVert \delta \rVert$, so the margin
stays positive while $\lVert \delta \rVert$ is below the ratio. For a whole encoder we summarize with
$\etaL$, where $\eta$ is the mean margin and $L$ the mean gradient size over correctly-classified clean
images. Both the per-image ratio and the summary have two properties that make them the right scalar.
They are threat-matched, since the dual norm matches the attack. And they are gauge-free: multiplying
all logits by a constant $c$ scales the margin and its gradient by the same $c$, so the ratio is
unchanged, which means a model's logit temperature cannot inflate or deflate it.

We make three claims. First and most important, within a fixed frozen encoder the per-image $\etaL$
orders the per-image certified adversarial radius more than twice as strongly as per-image
shift-consistency, and the gap is significant; because the comparison is within one encoder,
adversarial training cannot confound it. Second, across a panel of frozen encoders $\etaL$ also ranks
tower-level AutoAttack robustness, with the honest caveat that the number of adversarially robust
encoders is small, so the tower axis is supporting rather than load-bearing. Third, the apparent link
between shift-invariance and robustness is an artifact of weak attacks: on every non-robust encoder,
single-step FGSM reports robust accuracy that AutoAttack erases, a gradient-masking signature, so a
recent claim that architectural invariance confers robustness without adversarial training does not
survive a strong attack.

\paragraph{Predicting robustness without attacks.}
Two lines estimate a model's adversarial robustness without running the attack. GREAT Score~\citep{greatscore} derives a
global robustness statistic from a generative model of the data and correlates it with attack-based
rankings on RobustBench, and RDI~\citep{rdi} ranks classifiers by a feature-cluster separation index. Both rank
ordinary trained classifiers by a quantity that is neither a margin nor an input gradient, and neither
touches frozen foundation-model encoders, a per-image certified radius, or a paired invariance null. A
separate line uses the logit margin as a per-sample brittleness signal within a single fixed model~\citep{ngnawe};
this is the closest in spirit to our numerator, but it is a within-model detector on CIFAR classifiers,
not a cross-encoder ranking, and it does not divide by a Lipschitz term.

\paragraph{Margin, Lipschitz, and invariance in robustness.}
That a large margin over a small local Lipschitz constant implies a robust radius is classical, and
recent training methods add a margin term and a sensitivity term and report an improved
margin-to-sensitivity ratio~\citep{macs}. Those methods are training regularizers on supervised networks, and their
``consistency'' term is agreement under Gaussian noise or blur, not agreement under pixel shifts; they
do not use the ratio as an attack-free predictor across frozen towers, and they do not pair it with a
shift-consistency null. On the invariance side, prior work shows that shift-invariance can reduce
$\ell_\infty$ robustness by shrinking the margin~\citep{singlage}, which is exactly why we expect the consistency null,
and a separate result establishes a tradeoff between defenses against sensitivity-type and
invariance-type adversarial examples~\citep{tramer}. Our contribution is not to relate invariance and robustness in
general, which is well studied, but to show that a threat-matched margin ratio predicts robustness
while an invariance metric does not, in frozen vision-language encoders, and that
the apparent positive link is a weak-attack artifact.

\paragraph{Weak attacks and gradient masking.}
The gradient-masking literature established that a defense can look robust under weak attacks and fail
under strong ones, with single-step attacks beating iterative ones as a diagnostic~\citep{athalye,carlinieval}, and that strong
ensembles such as AutoAttack are the right arbiter~\citep{autoattack}. Our weak-attack section applies this diagnostic to
a specific recent claim that architectural invariance or equivariance confers robustness without
adversarial training, evaluated only with FGSM and PGD~\citep{equivariance}; we show the claimed link is a weak-attack
artifact on frozen vision-language towers.

\paragraph{Foundation-model robustness and certified language robustness.}
The adversarial robustness of a vision-language model is governed by its vision tower~\citep{towergoverns}, and
adversarially trained CLIP encoders such as FARE~\citep{fare} and TeCoA~\citep{tecoa} provide the robust
encoders in our panel; undefended CLIP is known to break completely under AutoAttack~\citep{pmgaft}, which
we use as the arbiter rather than rediscover.

\section{Background: the dissociation on image classifiers}
\label{sec:cifar}
% One paragraph + cite prior CIFAR work: eta/L predicts robustness (Pearson ~0.998 across arms),
% shift-consistency anti-predicts under AT (Linf -0.88), threat-matched eta/L predicts AutoAttack (+0.90);
% FGSM shows invariance helping, AutoAttack erases it. Sets up the generalization to foundation models.
The dissociation was first established on image classifiers, which we recap briefly as the origin of
the present study. Across a family of CIFAR-10 models that vary only in how much shift-invariance they
build in, the threat-matched $\etaL$ tracks robustness closely (Pearson around $+0.998$ under an
$\ell_2$ threat model on standard-trained arms), while shift-consistency is uninformative to
anti-predictive. Under adversarial training the split is sharper: shift-consistency anti-predicts
AutoAttack robustness (around $-0.88$ under an $\ell_\infty$ threat model), and the exactly invariant
arm, which has perfect consistency, is the least robust, while the threat-matched $\etaL$ still
predicts (around $+0.90$). The same two patterns hold across MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, a ResNet backbone,
and ImageNet-100, where clean accuracy is flat across arms so the difference is margin geometry rather
than underfitting. The natural question, which this paper answers, is whether these patterns are a
property of small convolutional classifiers or of learned representations more broadly.

\section{Vision-language encoders}
\label{sec:vlm}
We first ask whether \etaL{} predicts adversarial robustness across frozen vision-language towers,
and whether shift-consistency does. This is the setting where the two notions are most often
conflated, because an encoder that assigns nearby representations to shifted views of an image is
routinely described as robust.

\paragraph{Setup.}
We take a panel of eleven frozen vision towers used for zero-shot classification on ImageNet-100:
four adversarially trained encoders (FARE at two radii, TeCoA at two radii), a base CLIP tower, a
DINOv2 tower, and five further CLIP variants spanning pretraining corpus, patch size, and capacity.
For each tower we compute, on clean correctly-classified images only, the margin $M(x)$ of the
zero-shot logits, the ratio \etaL{} with $L$ the mean $\ell_1$ norm of $\nabla_x M$ (the dual norm of
an $\ell_\infty$ attack), and a shift-consistency score, the fraction of integer pixel shifts under
which the top-1 label is unchanged. We then measure adversarial robustness directly with AutoAttack,
reported as the APGD-CE plus APGD-T ensemble, both as a per-tower robust accuracy and as a per-image
certified radius from a fine grid. All quantities use the same set of clean, correctly-classified
images per tower, and \etaL{} is gauge-free, so a tower's logit temperature cannot affect it.

\paragraph{Across towers, \etaL{} tracks robustness, but the panel is small.}
The ratio \etaL{} correlates with per-tower robust accuracy at Pearson $+0.953$
($95\%$ CI $[0.92, 1.0]$, permutation $p=0.0009$). This number has to be read carefully. Of the eleven
encoders, only four are adversarially trained and the other seven sit at essentially zero robust
accuracy under AutoAttack, so any quantity that separates trained from ordinary encoders will correlate
with robustness. We remove an adversarial-training indicator as a covariate. The partial correlation is
$+0.51$ (Pearson) or $+0.31$ (rank-based), but with only four robust encoders neither is significant
(permutation $p=0.15$ and $0.39$). So the honest tower-level statement is that \etaL{} separates the
four robust encoders from the seven non-robust ones and orders the four in the right direction, but the
panel is too small to call the within-robust ordering significant; the load-bearing evidence is the
per-image axis below. A cosine-similarity form of consistency looks strong at first ($+0.81$) but
collapses to $+0.15$ under the same covariate, because it works only by detecting adversarial training,
and the prediction-agreement form of shift-consistency does not order robustness at all (Pearson
$+0.37$, not significant). Used as a selection rule on this panel, picking the encoder with the largest
\etaL{} lands within 5 points of the best encoder's robust accuracy, while picking by
prediction-agreement consistency or by clean accuracy lands 88 points away.

\paragraph{Per image, the dissociation holds within towers and is not an artifact of adversarial
training.}
The powered version of the claim is per image, within a fixed tower, where the trained-versus-untrained
split cannot act as a confound. In each of the four adversarially trained towers the per-image \etaL{}
orders the per-image robust radius at Spearman $+0.74$ to $+0.85$ (mean $+0.79$, $n=300$ per tower),
while per-image shift-consistency orders it far less, at $+0.27$ to $+0.37$ (mean $+0.33$). Consistency
is therefore not uninformative about the per-image radius, but the threat-matched ratio orders it more
than twice as strongly. With a finer radius grid the ordinary CLIP towers also acquire a real spread of
per-image radii, and within each of them \etaL{} orders the radius at Spearman between $+0.54$ and
$+0.79$, every confidence interval above zero. The per-image dissociation therefore holds inside
ordinary, non-robust towers as well, so it is not a byproduct of the trained-versus-untrained split. We
report the per-tower ranges rather than a single pooled number, because pooling images across towers
with a coarse, tied radius grid distorts the weaker consistency signal.

\paragraph{What survives and what to state honestly.}
The implementation was checked line by line and no result changes sign under any check; there is no
gradient masking, since APGD $\leq$ PGD-40 $\leq$ FGSM and a black-box Square attack does not beat
APGD on any tower. The honest limitation is that the seven non-robust towers all sit at essentially
zero robust accuracy under strong attack, so the range of tower-level robustness comes from the
trained towers; the covariate control removes the confound in the ranking, and the powered claim rests
on the per-image axis, which stands on both trained and ordinary towers.

\section{The invariance-robustness correlation is a weak-attack artifact}
\label{sec:weakattack}
A recent line of work argues that architectural invariance or equivariance confers adversarial
robustness on its own, without adversarial training, and reports this using single-step FGSM and
short PGD. One representative paper attributes the effect to gradient regularization and smoother
decision boundaries and never runs a strong attack. We show that on frozen, non-adversarially-trained
vision-language towers this apparent link is an artifact of weak attacks.

\paragraph{Setup and the diagnostic we apply.}
The standard way to catch a false sense of robustness is the gradient-masking checklist: a single-step
attack should not beat an iterative one, robust accuracy should fall as attack iterations increase, and
a black-box attack should not beat a white-box one. We run, on each of the seven non-robust towers and
the four adversarially trained towers as a contrast, FGSM and PGD at 2, 5, 10, 20, and 40 steps, then
the APGD-CE plus APGD-T AutoAttack ensemble and a black-box Square attack, all at $\varepsilon=2/255$.
For each attack strength we also correlate the tower's shift-consistency with its robust accuracy
across towers, which is the quantity the pro-invariance claim relies on.

\paragraph{Result.}
On every non-robust tower, single-step FGSM reports $16$ to $61\%$ robust accuracy (mean $0.38$ at
$\varepsilon=2/255$), which falls to exactly $0.0$ under both PGD-40 and AutoAttack, while the four
adversarially trained towers stay robust (AutoAttack robust accuracy $0.75$ to $0.88$). The quantity
the pro-invariance claim relies on is the correlation, across towers, between shift-consistency and
robust accuracy. Across the seven non-robust towers that correlation is Pearson $+0.82$ when robustness
is measured with FGSM: more shift-consistent towers do look more robust. Under AutoAttack every one of
those towers sits at $0.0$, so there is no robustness left to order and the correlation is not present.
The ``more invariant is more robust'' ordering therefore exists only while the attack is weak, which is
the gradient-masking signature that a single-step attack overstates robustness. We report this as a
targeted correction of the specific pro-invariance claim, not as a discovery that undefended encoders
are fragile, which is already known.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
The through-line is that shift-invariance and adversarial robustness are different properties of a
frozen encoder, and that a threat-matched margin-to-Lipschitz ratio, not an invariance metric, is what
tracks robustness. The practical reading for a vision-language practitioner is to judge a per-image
adversarial radius, or choose between encoders, by $\etaL$ rather than by shift-consistency, since
consistency orders the per-image radius far less well and, as a selection rule on our panel, lands 88
points of robust accuracy from the best encoder. A second reading is to distrust any claim that more
invariance means more robustness when it has only been checked with a weak attack, since that ordering
does not survive AutoAttack.

Several limitations bound the claims. The tower axis is small: only four of our encoders are
adversarially robust, so the range of tower-level robustness comes from them and the covariate-controlled
tower correlation is not significant at this panel size. The load-bearing evidence is therefore the
per-image axis, which is within a fixed encoder and holds inside ordinary encoders as well; expanding
the set of adversarially robust encoders would let the tower axis carry more weight, and is the natural
next step. Consistency is not useless for the per-image radius, ordering it at about $+0.33$; our claim
is the quantitative one that $\etaL$ orders it more than twice as strongly, not that invariance carries
no signal. Finally, the certificate reading of $\etaL$ is first-order and tight for a single, near-linear
decision such as a zero-shot classification, and we do not claim it transfers to settings where the
object being attacked is not a single decision.
