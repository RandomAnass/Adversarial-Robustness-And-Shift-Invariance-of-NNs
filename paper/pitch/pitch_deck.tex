  \begin{center}\small\color{slate}
    A model that ignores small shifts of its input is called invariant. A model that does not flip
    under a tiny worst-case change is called robust. We show these are different, measure what tells
    them apart, and correct a common mistake.
  \end{center}
\end{frame}}

%==============================================================================
% THE PUZZLE
%==============================================================================
\begin{frame}{The puzzle}{Does making a vision model more shift-invariant help or hurt its adversarial robustness?}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{beamercolorbox}[sep=8pt,rounded=true]{block body}
        \small \warmt{\textbf{One literature says: it helps.}}\\[0.3em]
        Anti-aliasing and equivariant designs report better robustness. Smoother, more invariant
        models look safer.
      \end{beamercolorbox}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{beamercolorbox}[sep=8pt,rounded=true]{block body}
        \small \bad{\textbf{Another says: it hurts.}}\\[0.3em]
        Perfect shift-invariance can shrink the margin between classes, which should make attacks
        easier.
      \end{beamercolorbox}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \vspace{0.8em}
  \begin{center}\lead{Both can't be right as stated. Which is it, and what is the real signal?}\end{center}
  \takeaway{We resolve this with one simple quantity, and find that the field's usual invariance
  metric is the wrong thing to look at.}
\end{frame}

%==============================================================================
% TWO DIFFERENT THINGS
%==============================================================================
\begin{frame}{Two different things, often confused}{}
  \vspace{0.8em}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      {\bf\large \acc{Invariance}}\\[0.4em]
      Ignoring changes that should not matter: a small shift, a crop, a reworded prompt.\\[0.5em]
      Measured by \emph{shift-consistency}: how often the prediction stays the same when you shift
      the image.
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      {\bf\large \good{Adversarial robustness}}\\[0.4em]
      Not being flipped by a small \emph{worst-case} change chosen by an attacker.\\[0.5em]
      Measured by running a strong attack (AutoAttack) and seeing how big a perturbation it takes to
      change the answer.
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \vspace{0.9em}
  \begin{center}\small\color{slate}
    They sound related, and the field often uses the first as a proxy for the second.
    Our first finding is that this proxy does not work.
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

%==============================================================================
% THE MEASURING STICK
%==============================================================================
\begin{frame}{The measuring stick: a margin-to-sensitivity ratio}{$\eta/L$, computed on clean images, no attack needed}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{center}
  $\displaystyle \etaL \;=\; \frac{\text{margin}}{\text{sensitivity}}
   \;=\; \frac{\text{how confident the model is}}{\text{how fast that confidence changes if you nudge the input}}$
  \end{center}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  \begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
    \item The \keyword{margin} is the gap between the top class and the runner-up.
    \item The \keyword{sensitivity} is the size of the input gradient of that margin, measured in the
      way the attacker moves (the norm matched to the threat).
    \item A large ratio means: far from the decision boundary, and slow to get there. That is exactly
      what robustness should be.
  \end{itemize}
  \takeaway{$\eta/L$ is a first-order estimate of how big a perturbation it takes to flip the label.
  It needs no attack and is not changed by rescaling the model's outputs.}
\end{frame}

%==============================================================================
% FINDING 1: SATURATION
%==============================================================================
\begin{frame}{Finding 1: the invariance metric is saturated}{Shift-consistency looks the same for every modern model, robust or not}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{center}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{lcc}
    \toprule
     & shift-consistency & spread across models\\
    \midrule
    16 frozen CLIP encoders & \bad{$0.96$ -- $0.99$} & almost none\\
    (robust and non-robust alike) & & \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  \begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
    \item Every modern encoder is already near-perfectly shift-consistent, so the metric cannot tell
      them apart on \emph{any} kind of robustness.
    \item Once you account for clean accuracy, it does not predict robustness to common corruptions
      either.
    \item The same squeeze appears on strong CNN backbones: consistency stops discriminating.
  \end{itemize}
  \takeaway{Shift-consistency is treated as a robustness signal, but it is saturated. It has no room
  left to rank models. This is why the ``invariance predicts robustness'' story keeps giving mixed answers.}
\end{frame}

%==============================================================================
% FINDING 2: WEAK-ATTACK ARTIFACT
%==============================================================================
\begin{frame}{Finding 2: ``invariance helps'' is a weak-attack artifact}{The effect shows up under a cheap attack and vanishes under a strong one}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
      \begin{itemize}\footnotesize\itemsep4pt
        \item Under a single-step FGSM attack, more invariant models look more robust: the
          ``invariance helps'' ordering appears (left panel, a real trend).
        \item Under AutoAttack, every non-robust encoder drops to \good{zero}. There is no robustness
          left to order, and the trend disappears (right panel).
        \item A weak attack simply overstates robustness, the classic gradient-masking signature.
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.46\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{../../llm_transfer/paper/figures/fig3_weakattack.pdf}\\[0.2em]
      {\scriptsize\color{slate} Same encoders, same budget: shift-consistency looks like it predicts
      robustness under FGSM, and predicts nothing under AutoAttack.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \takeaway{Recent claims that architectural invariance gives robustness without adversarial training
  are measured with weak attacks. They do not survive a strong one.}
\end{frame}

%==============================================================================
% FINDING 3: eta/L PREDICTS
%==============================================================================
\begin{frame}{Finding 3: the margin ratio predicts robustness}{$\eta/L$ orders robustness where invariance does not, across three settings}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.5\textwidth}
      \footnotesize
      \begin{tabular}{lcc}
        \toprule
        setting & \good{$\eta/L$} & \bad{consistency}\\
        \midrule
        CIFAR-10 classifiers   & \good{$+0.99$} & \bad{uninformative}\\
        ResNet, ImageNet-100   & \good{$+0.90$} & \bad{anti-corr.}\\
        CLIP, per image        & \good{$+0.74$--$0.96$} & \bad{$\approx 0$}\\
        \bottomrule
      \end{tabular}
      \vspace{0.6em}
      \begin{itemize}\footnotesize\itemsep3pt
        \item The same law from small CNNs to a ResNet to frozen foundation-model encoders.
        \item Inside one robust encoder, image by image, $\eta/L$ tracks how large an attack each image
          needs; shift-consistency does not.
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{../../llm_transfer/paper/figures/fig2_perimage.pdf}\\[0.2em]
      {\scriptsize\color{slate} Per image, within a robust CLIP encoder: \good{$\eta/L$ (left)} orders
      the attack size, \bad{shift-consistency (right)} does not.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \takeaway{The right question is not ``how invariant is the model'' but ``how large is the margin
  relative to the sensitivity''. That is $\eta/L$.}
\end{frame}

%==============================================================================
% THE THEORY: two-sided bracket
%==============================================================================
\begin{frame}{Why it works: a two-sided bracket}{The ratio is not just a heuristic; it brackets the true robust radius}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  \begin{center}
  \begin{tikzpicture}[every node/.style={font=\small}]
    \draw[-{Latex[length=2mm]},slate,line width=1pt] (-0.3,0)--(9.5,0);
    \node[slate] at (9.9,0) {size};
    \draw[good,line width=2.2pt] (2,-0.12)--(2,0.12); \node[good,above=2pt] at (2,0.14) {$\eta/L$};
    \node[good,below=3pt,font=\scriptsize] at (2,-0.14) {certified safe};
    \draw[accent,line width=2.6pt] (4.6,-0.16)--(4.6,0.16); \node[accent,above=2pt] at (4.6,0.18) {$r$};
    \node[accent,below=3pt,font=\scriptsize] at (4.6,-0.18) {true radius};
    \draw[warm,line width=2.2pt] (7,-0.12)--(7,0.12); \node[warm,above=2pt] at (7,0.14) {$\eta/\alpha$};
    \node[warm,below=3pt,font=\scriptsize] at (7,-0.14) {must be unsafe};
    \draw[decorate,decoration={brace,amplitude=4pt},slate] (2,0.55)--(7,0.55);
    \node[slate,font=\scriptsize] at (4.5,0.95) {gap $=$ condition number $\kappa=L/\alpha$};
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{0.4em}
  \begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
    \item The \keyword{lower bound} is the standard certificate: the model cannot be flipped by
      anything smaller than $\eta/L$.
    \item The \keyword{upper bound} is the matching side: with $\alpha$ a slope along the path to the
      boundary, no perturbation larger than $\eta/\alpha$ can be safe.
    \item The gap between the two is a condition number $\kappa = L/\alpha$. When it is close to one,
      $\eta/L$ is not just a bound, it is the answer.
  \end{itemize}
  \takeaway{$\eta/L$ is the small-curvature limit of the true radius. This tells us exactly when it is
  tight, and points to what it misses.}
\end{frame}

%==============================================================================
% FINDING 4a: detect vs rank
%==============================================================================
\begin{frame}{Finding 4: detecting robustness is not the same as ranking it}{$\eta/L$ separates robust from fragile, but cannot order the robust ones}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  \begin{itemize}\itemsep5pt
    \item Across all encoders, $\eta/L$ cleanly \good{separates} the robust ones from the fragile ones.
    \item But \emph{among the already-robust} encoders, $\eta/L$ no longer orders them. Two encoders
      with the same $\eta/L$ can differ in robustness.
    \item Something beyond the margin and the size of the gradient is at play.
  \end{itemize}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  \begin{center}\lead{So what ranks the robust models?}\end{center}
  \takeaway{$\eta/L$ uses the \emph{size} of the gradient. To rank robust models we need its
  \emph{shape}.}
\end{frame}

%==============================================================================
% FINDING 4b: anisotropy
%==============================================================================
\begin{frame}{Finding 4: the shape of the gradient ranks robust models}{A concentrated gradient resists an all-pixel attack better than a spread one}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.54\textwidth}
      \begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
        \item An $\ell_\infty$ attack pushes on \emph{every} pixel at once.
        \item If the margin's gradient is \good{concentrated} on a few pixels, most of that budget is
          wasted, and the model holds.
        \item If it is \bad{spread} over many pixels, the attack engages all of them, and the model
          gives way.
        \item Measured by the gradient's \keyword{anisotropy} (how spread it is), this orders the
          robust encoders where $\eta/L$ could not.
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.44\textwidth}
      \centering
      \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.52,every node/.style={font=\scriptsize}]
        % concentrated gradient (robust)
        \draw[good,very thick,rounded corners] (0,0) rectangle (3,3);
        \foreach \x in {0,1,2}\foreach \y in {0,1,2}\draw[rulec] (\x,\y) rectangle (\x+1,\y+1);
        \draw[good,-{Latex[length=3mm]},line width=2.2pt] (1.5,1.5)--(2.35,1.5);
        \node[good] at (1.5,-0.5) {concentrated};
        \node[good] at (1.5,-1.05) {\bf robust};
        % spread gradient (fragile)
        \begin{scope}[xshift=4.2cm]
        \draw[bad,very thick,rounded corners] (0,0) rectangle (3,3);
        \foreach \x in {0,1,2}\foreach \y in {0,1,2}{\draw[rulec] (\x,\y) rectangle (\x+1,\y+1);
          \draw[bad,-{Latex[length=1.6mm]},line width=0.9pt] (\x+0.5,\y+0.5)--(\x+0.85,\y+0.5);}
        \node[bad] at (1.5,-0.5) {spread};
        \node[bad] at (1.5,-1.05) {\bf fragile};
        \end{scope}
      \end{tikzpicture}\\[1.3em]
      {\scriptsize\color{slate} An all-pixel attack wastes its budget on a concentrated gradient,
      but exploits every arrow of a spread one.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \vspace{0.3em}
  {\footnotesize\color{slate} There is a clean case where this is provable: when the curvature lines up
  with the gradient, a more spread gradient shrinks the robust radius. Whether that is the exact
  mechanism in real encoders is still open; the ranking itself is robust to every control we tried.}
  \takeaway{Robustness among strong models is about \emph{where} the model is sensitive, not only how
  much. Gradient shape is the missing piece; the reason it works is still being pinned down.}
\end{frame}

%==============================================================================
% FINDING 5: sign-flip
%==============================================================================
\begin{frame}{Finding 5: adversarial training flips a hidden relationship}{A fingerprint of what robust training actually changes}
  \vspace{0.7em}
  \begin{itemize}\itemsep5pt
    \item In an ordinary model, the images with the largest margin tend to have the \emph{largest}
      gradients: confidence and sensitivity rise together.
    \item After adversarial training, this reverses: high-margin images now have the \emph{smallest}
      gradients.
    \item The per-image relationship between margin and sensitivity flips sign, consistently across
      dozens of models.
  \end{itemize}
  \takeaway{Robust training does not just raise the margin. It re-couples margin and sensitivity, and
  $\eta/L$ is the quantity that reads this change.}
\end{frame}

%==============================================================================
% WHAT IT MEANS
%==============================================================================
\begin{frame}{What it all means}{}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{itemize}\itemsep6pt
    \item[\acc{1.}] \keyword{Stop using shift-consistency as a robustness signal.} It is saturated on
      modern models and does not order adversarial robustness.
    \item[\acc{2.}] \keyword{Distrust ``invariance helps'' claims tested with weak attacks.} They
      vanish under AutoAttack.
    \item[\acc{3.}] \keyword{Use the margin-to-sensitivity ratio $\eta/L$.} It predicts robustness
      without running an attack, from CNNs to frozen CLIP encoders.
    \item[\acc{4.}] \keyword{To compare strong models, look at the gradient's shape,} not only its
      size. Concentrated sensitivity is more robust.
  \end{itemize}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{center}\small\color{slate}
    One idea, the margin relative to the sensitivity, unifies a split literature and turns a vague
    intuition about invariance into something you can measure and predict.
  \end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The three-beat story}{}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  \begin{enumerate}\itemsep8pt
    \item[\acc{Beat 1.}] \textbf{The confusion.} People treat invariance and robustness as the same
      thing and get contradictory results. They are not the same thing.
    \item[\acc{Beat 2.}] \textbf{The fix.} A simple ratio, margin over sensitivity, predicts
      robustness without any attack, and the field's invariance metric does not, because it is saturated.
    \item[\acc{Beat 3.}] \textbf{The refinement.} That ratio tells robust from fragile; to rank the
      robust ones you also need the shape of the model's sensitivity, and the apparent ``invariance
      helps'' effect is just a weak-attack mirage.
  \end{enumerate}
  \takeaway{If you remember one line: \emph{invariance is not robustness; the margin relative to the
  sensitivity is.}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two analogies and the questions you will get}{}
  \vspace{0.4em}
  \begin{itemize}\itemsep5pt
    \item \keyword{Analogy 1.} A locked door is not a strong door. Ignoring a gentle push (invariance)
      is not the same as withstanding a crowbar (an attack).
    \item \keyword{Analogy 2.} Two people equally far from a cliff edge: the one who moves slowly toward
      it is safer. That ``distance over speed'' is $\eta/L$.
  \end{itemize}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  \begin{beamercolorbox}[sep=7pt,rounded=true]{block body}
    \footnotesize
    \textbf{Q: Isn't the ratio just re-describing the radius it predicts?}\ Partly, and we say so;
    the surprise is that the invariance metric fails where the ratio succeeds.\\[0.3em]
    \textbf{Q: Does it hold on big models?}\ Yes, on frozen CLIP encoders as well as CNNs.\\[0.3em]
    \textbf{Q: Can you optimize it directly?}\ No, it is a diagnostic, not a training target; forcing
    it up collapses the margin.
  \end{beamercolorbox}
\end{frame}
